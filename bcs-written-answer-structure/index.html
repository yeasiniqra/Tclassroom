Written Exam: Structuring Answers That Score

In the Written exam, two candidates can know the same amount and score forty marks apart. The difference is almost never knowledge. It is structure, and structure can be learned.

The Written examination carries 900 marks and asks you to produce, not to recognise. An examiner marking hundreds of scripts spends only a few minutes on yours. Everything below is about making those minutes work in your favour: showing what you know quickly, in an order the examiner expects.

What changes after Preliminary

In the Preliminary, the correct option is on the page and you find it. In the Written, nothing is on the page except what you put there. Two skills matter that the MCQ stage never tested: selecting what to include, and arranging it so a marker can follow. Candidates who carry MCQ habits into the Written tend to write everything they remember in the order they remember it, and lose marks for answers that are full but shapeless.

The shape of a good answer

Almost every broad question fits the same three-part frame. Use it until it becomes automatic.

  • Introduction · 10–15% — define the key term, state the scope, and signal your line of argument. Three or four sentences. No throat-clearing.
  • Body · 70–80% — three to five labelled points, each a short paragraph. One idea per paragraph, its claim in the first sentence.
  • Conclusion · 10–15% — answer the question directly. Do not summarise what you just wrote; state what follows from it.

The single highest-value habit: put your point in the first sentence of each paragraph, then support it. An examiner skimming only your opening sentences should still be able to reconstruct your whole answer.

A worked skeleton

Take a typical question: “Evaluate the role of remittance in Bangladesh’s economic development.” Before writing a word, sketch this in the margin:

  1. Intro — define remittance; note its scale relative to GDP and export earnings; state that its role is significant but uneven.
  2. Contribution — foreign exchange reserves, balance of payments, rural household consumption.
  3. Social effect — education and health spending in receiving households; changes in rural labour.
  4. Limitations — dependence on a few destination countries, informal transfer channels, low investment share.
  5. Way forward — skills training before migration, cheaper formal channels, diversifying destinations.
  6. Conclusion — remittance is a pillar, not a strategy; its value depends on how it is channelled.

That skeleton takes two minutes and prevents the most common failure: writing three strong paragraphs, running out of time, and never reaching the evaluation the question actually asked for.

Evidence, data and diagrams

A specific figure is worth a paragraph of general praise. Carry a small stock of current numbers — GDP growth, per-capita income, literacy, poverty rate, budget size — and use them where they belong. Where a relationship is easier drawn than described, draw it. A clean flow diagram or a simple table breaks the wall of text and shows the examiner you have organised the material, not just recalled it. Label it, keep it small, and reference it in the text.

Examiners do not award marks for effort. They award them for points they can find. Make every point findable.

Language and presentation

  • Short sentences. A complicated idea in plain words reads as mastery; a simple idea in ornate words reads as padding.
  • Underline key terms and headings, sparingly. Two underlines per page guide the eye; ten defeat the purpose.
  • Leave a margin and a blank line between points. White space makes an answer look organised before it is even read.
  • Write legibly. A marker who has to decipher a sentence will not pause to be generous.

Time management in the hall

Divide the total minutes by the total marks before you start, and give each question its share. The most expensive mistake in the Written is a beautifully developed first answer followed by two unfinished ones. An answer at 70% of its potential scores far more than an answer left at 0%, so move on when your allotted time ends, and return only if minutes remain at the end.

How to practise

  1. Write full answers by hand, timed. Planning answers mentally builds none of the required speed.
  2. Have someone mark them against the question, not against your knowledge.
  3. Rewrite one previously marked answer each week using the feedback. Repetition with correction is what changes the score.

Structure is the cheapest marks available in the Written exam. It costs no extra reading — only the discipline to arrange what you already know before you begin writing.
