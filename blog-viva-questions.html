Viva Board: 20 Questions You Should Prepare

The viva carries 200 marks and is the only stage where the board meets you rather than your script. It rewards composure and honesty far more than encyclopaedic recall.

    Academic Team
  • 28 July 2026
  • 5 min read

On this page

  1. What the board is assessing
  2. About you and your subject
  3. Your district and background
  4. Cadre choice and service
  5. Current affairs and opinion
  6. When you do not know
  7. Etiquette that matters
Topper's

By the time you reach the viva, the board already knows you can pass written examinations. What they are testing now is judgement, temperament and honesty under mild pressure — whether you are someone they would be comfortable placing in a district office. Prepare for that, not for a quiz.

What the board is assessing

Members are looking for a small number of things, and almost every question is a route to one of them: depth in your own subject, awareness of the country you propose to serve, a defensible reason for your cadre choice, and how you behave when you are wrong or unsure. A confident “I do not know, sir” damages you far less than a confident invention.

About you and your subject

Your own name, subject and background are the safest marks in the room, and the most embarrassing to lose. Prepare these thoroughly:

  1. Tell us about yourself, in brief.
  2. What does your name mean, and were you named after anyone?
  3. Why did you study your honours subject?
  4. Define your subject, and name its most important branch.
  5. Which subject did you enjoy least, and why?
  6. Name a recent development in your field.
  7. How will your subject help you as an administrator?

Expect follow-ups. Boards rarely accept the first answer. Whatever you name, be ready to be asked about it twice more — so name something you genuinely know.

Your district and background

Questions about your home district are almost guaranteed. They test local awareness and, indirectly, whether you pay attention to the place you come from.

  1. Why is your district notable — historically or economically?
  2. Name the rivers, upazilas and neighbouring districts.
  3. Which famous person comes from your district?
  4. What is the main problem facing your district, and how would you address it?
  5. Name a Liberation War event connected to your area.

Cadre choice and service

This is where boards probe hardest, because a weak answer suggests you chose by prestige rather than intent. Have a reason that connects your background to the work.

  1. Why this cadre as your first choice?
  2. If you are not selected for it, will you join another?
  3. What are the main responsibilities of the post you are applying for?
  4. What would you do if a lawful instruction conflicted with your personal view?

The board is not trying to catch you out. They are trying to find out who you are when you are not certain. Answer as that person.

Current affairs and opinion

Read a national daily for the three months before your viva and keep a short note of the major stories. Opinion questions have no fixed answer — they test whether you can hold a position calmly.

  1. What is the most important national issue at present?
  2. Explain a recent government policy and your view of it.
  3. Name a current international event affecting Bangladesh.
  4. What is the greatest challenge facing the country over the next decade?

When you do not know

You will be asked something you cannot answer — often deliberately. The board is watching your response, not your knowledge. Say plainly that you do not know, offer what you do know if it is genuinely related, and stop. Do not guess at a fact, do not pad the silence, and do not let one blank answer change your composure for the rest of the interview.

Etiquette that matters

  • Ask permission before entering and before sitting.
  • Address the answer to the member who asked, but include the board.
  • Formal, simple dress. Nothing that invites a question of its own.
  • Carry your documents in order — fumbling reads as disorganisation.
  • Thank the board and leave without hurrying, whatever you feel it went like.

Prepare these twenty questions properly and you will have covered most of what a board asks. The remaining marks come from something you cannot rehearse: answering like someone who is ready for the responsibility.
