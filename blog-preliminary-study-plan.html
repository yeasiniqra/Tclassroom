How to Build a 6-Month BCS Preliminary Study Plan

Most candidates do not fail the Preliminary because they lack ability. They fail because six months of effort gets spread across the syllabus unevenly. Here is a plan that fixes that.

The BCS Preliminary is a 200-mark MCQ exam with a negative marking of 0.50 for every wrong answer. Six months is enough time to cover it properly, but only if you decide in advance where those hours go. A plan you write once and actually follow is worth more than a perfect plan you rewrite every week.

Why six months

Six months is long enough to read every subject twice and short enough to stay urgent. Shorter than four months and you will be forced to skip topics; longer than eight and the early chapters fade before the exam arrives. The structure below assumes roughly five to six focused hours a day — realistic for a full-time student, demanding but achievable alongside a job.

Know the marks first

Before planning a single day, memorise the mark distribution. Study time should follow marks, not interest. Candidates routinely spend weeks on General Science, worth 15 marks, while giving Bangla and English — worth 70 between them — a fraction of that attention.

Read that again: Bangla, English and Bangladesh Affairs together carry 100 of the 200 marks. Half your total study time belongs to these three subjects.

The three phases

Split the six months into three unequal blocks. Each has one job, and only one.

Months 1–3 · Coverage

Read every subject once, end to end, without chasing perfection. Take short notes in your own words, no more than a page per chapter. Do not attempt full model tests yet — you will only confirm that you have not finished the syllabus, which you already know.

Months 4–5 · Consolidation

Second reading, this time with previous years’ questions open beside the book. Solve the last ten BCS Preliminary papers subject by subject. Patterns appear quickly: certain chapters produce questions almost every year, and those deserve a third reading.

Month 6 · Retrieval

Stop reading new material. Full-length model tests under exam conditions, followed by careful review. Your only goal now is converting what you already know into marks under time pressure.

A realistic daily routine

A plan fails when it ignores energy. Put the subject you find hardest in your sharpest hours, not the one you enjoy most.

  • Morning · 2 hours — Bangla or English. Highest marks, highest concentration.
  • Midday · 1 hour — Mathematical Reasoning or Mental Ability. These need practice, not reading.
  • Afternoon · 1.5 hours — Bangladesh Affairs or International Affairs.
  • Evening · 1 hour — Current affairs, plus revision of yesterday’s notes.
  • One full day each week — no new material. Revise the week and sit one sectional test.

Revision is not what you do after studying. It is the part that turns studying into marks. Schedule it first, then fit new reading around it.

Model tests, done properly

A model test is only useful if you review it. The score matters far less than the reason behind each wrong answer. After every test, sort your mistakes into three groups:

  1. Did not know — a genuine gap. Go back to the book and note the chapter.
  2. Knew it but misread — an attention error. These fade with more timed practice.
  3. Guessed wrong — a negative-marking problem, not a knowledge problem.

With 0.50 negative marking, a blind guess costs you on average. If you can eliminate two of the four options, answering becomes worthwhile; if you cannot eliminate any, leave it blank. Candidates lose more marks to undisciplined guessing than to any single weak subject.

Five costly mistakes

  • Collecting more books instead of finishing one. Two good books read twice beat six read once.
  • Leaving Mathematical Reasoning until the last month. It rewards steady practice, never cramming.
  • Reading current affairs without writing anything down. Six months of headlines will not stay.
  • Skipping previous years’ questions until the end. They are the syllabus, made specific.
  • Studying eleven hours on Friday and none on Tuesday. Consistency beats intensity, every time.

The final 30 days

In the last month, narrow rather than broaden. Revise only your own notes and previous years’ questions. Sit a full model test every second day, at the same hour as the real exam, so your concentration peaks at the right time of day. Sleep properly in the final week — recall degrades faster from lost sleep than from one missed chapter.

A six-month plan is not about studying more than everyone else. It is about making sure the hours you do put in land on the marks that actually appear on the paper.
